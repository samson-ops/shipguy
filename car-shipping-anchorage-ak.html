Everything about shipping a car to Alaska is different, starting with the map: no auto transport truck drives from the Lower 48 to Anchorage. Your car crosses on a ship. Understanding that one fact — and the ocean-freight rules that come with it — is the difference between a smooth two-week move and a car sitting refused at a Tacoma terminal.

Quick answerShipping a car between Anchorage and the Lower 48 is a two-leg trip: a highway leg to or from the Tacoma gateway, then an ocean sailing to the Port of Alaska. All-in for a sedan: $1,600–$2,300 to or from the Seattle area, $2,000–$3,300 from farther out, taking 1–3.5 weeks door to door depending on distance and sailing schedules. The ocean leg has rules highway shipping doesn't — empty vehicle, quarter tank — all explained below. Get a two-leg quote in minutes.

Ship Guy coordinates the whole chain from our office at Ship Creek, in sight of the port that receives nearly everything Anchorage owns: the highway leg anywhere in the Lower 48, the sailing north, and the final delivery across Southcentral Alaska or up to Fairbanks. One booking, one point of contact, both directions.

This page explains how the system actually works — the legs, the sailing schedules, the empty-vehicle rule, winter prep, the ALCAN question everyone asks, and what military families PCSing to JBER need to know. If you read nothing else, read the ocean-leg rules; they’re the part that surprises people.

How Shipping a Car to Alaska Actually Works

Leg one: the highway. A standard auto carrier picks up your vehicle anywhere in the Lower 48 and runs it to the ocean gateway at the Port of Tacoma — a day from anywhere on the West Coast, up to a week from the East. From the Seattle–Tacoma area itself, this leg all but disappears, which is why the Pacific Northwest is the cheapest place in America to ship to Alaska from.

Leg two: the water. Ocean carriers sail the Tacoma–Anchorage run on fixed weekly schedules, roll-on/roll-off or trailer-borne, arriving at the Port of Alaska a short walk from our office. The crossing plus port handling on both ends runs about four to eight days — and here’s the planning insight most people miss: your total transit time depends less on when you book than on which sailing your car catches. Miss a Thursday cutoff by a day and the car waits nearly a week for the next vessel. We plan the highway leg backward from the sailing schedule so your car arrives at the terminal with margin, not with a prayer.

Leg three: the last miles. Once the vessel discharges and the port releases the vehicle, we deliver across Anchorage, the Mat-Su, the Kenai, or hand it to the Fairbanks connector. Going the other direction — leaving Alaska — the same chain runs in reverse, and southbound sailings follow the same weekly rhythm.

The container option. There is no such thing as an enclosed trailer across the Gulf of Alaska, but there is its maritime cousin: containerized shipping, where your vehicle rides secured inside a sealed container. It costs meaningfully more and suits the same cars enclosed trailers do — collectors, classics, anything you’d rather not expose to salt air on deck. Ask and we’ll price both.

The Ocean-Leg Rules: Read These Before You Pack the Trunk

Highway carriers in the Lower 48 will usually tolerate a hundred pounds of belongings below the window line. Ocean carriers will not, and the difference catches Alaska shippers off guard every single week.

The vehicle ships empty. Completely. Maritime regulations and carrier policy prohibit personal goods in the vehicle — not a box of books, not the winter tires in the trunk, not the moving-day overflow. Vehicles are inspected at the terminal before acceptance, and one that fails inspection gets refused, which means missing its sailing and waiting for the next one. Ship your household goods as household goods; ship the car as a car.

Fuel at a quarter tank or less. A hard maritime rule, stricter than the highway norm. Arrive at the gateway above it and someone has to burn fuel off before the terminal will take the vehicle — an annoying, avoidable delay.

Keys, alarms, and honesty about condition. The terminal needs a working key, any alarm disabled or documented, and truthful notes on quirks — a car that won’t restart on the vessel’s deck in Anchorage becomes a winch job with an audience. Non-runners ship fine, but only when declared up front so the right equipment is waiting on both ends.

Photograph everything at the first handoff. Your car will be handled more times than on any highway move — loaded, sailed, discharged, delivered. Condition documentation happens at each handoff, and your own dated photos from day one are the anchor the whole paper trail hangs on. Rare as claims are, this is the trip to be thorough about.

Winter Shipping: Preparing a Car for the Cold Soak

The ships sail all winter and the system runs year-round — Alaska doesn’t close. What changes in the cold months is what your vehicle needs to survive its first night outdoors at the destination, because a car that left Dallas at seventy degrees will meet Anchorage at ten below with whatever fluids and battery it brought along.

Before a winter shipment north, have a shop confirm four things: antifreeze protection to at least minus forty (Southern coolant mixes often aren’t), a battery that tests strong — the marginal battery that started fine in Phoenix will be a paperweight after a week of cold soak, winter-rated washer fluid (summer fluid freezes solid and can crack the reservoir), and tires that match the season — all-seasons at minimum, and if you’re staying, this is the state where real winter tires earn their keep. None of this is exotic; it’s the same list every Alaskan runs each October, applied a few weeks early.

Schedule-wise, deep winter adds slack rather than drama: storms in the Gulf of Alaska can slide a sailing, and snow can slow the last-mile delivery by a day. We build winter shipments with that margin included, and we’ll never promise you a January date the Gulf hasn’t agreed to yet.

The ALCAN Question: Shouldn’t I Just Drive It?

Every Alaska shipper asks, and it’s a fair question with a romantic answer and an accounting answer. The romantic answer: the Alaska Highway is one of the great drives on Earth, and if you have a week, a sense of adventure, and a vehicle you want another 2,200 miles on, do it once and keep the photos forever.

The accounting answer is less kind. Seattle to Anchorage overland means 2,200-plus miles, four to six nights of lodging, tanks upon tanks of fuel at remote-highway prices, meals, two border crossings, stone-chip and windshield risk on the gravel-patched stretches, and a week of your life — totaled honestly, it lands at or above the cost of the barge while adding real wear to the odometer. Hired driveaway services price into the same range and hand the wheel of your car to a stranger for a week. Seasonally, the calculation gets worse: the highway in winter is a serious undertaking that no one should book casually to save money they probably aren’t saving.

Our honest guidance after pricing this hundreds of times: drive the ALCAN for the experience or ship the car for the logistics — but don’t drive it for the savings, because for most people, most of the year, they aren’t there.

What Moves the Price of an Alaska Shipment

Lower 48 pricing is mostly mileage and season. Alaska pricing has its own physics, and knowing the levers helps you read any quote — ours included.

Dimensions rule the water. On a highway trailer, a lifted truck is a mild surcharge; on a vessel, where vehicles are stowed by measured footprint and height, size drives the ocean fare directly. A roof rack, a lift kit, an overheight camper shell — each one is honest information your quote needs up front, because the terminal will measure regardless and re-rate anything undeclared.

The land leg scales normally; the ocean leg doesn’t. That’s why Anchorage–Seattle and Anchorage–Portland sit close together while Anchorage–New York climbs: the sailing costs what it costs, and only the highway miles vary. It’s also why consolidating a move to a Pacific Northwest handoff — say, timing your shipment while visiting family in Washington — is the one genuinely large saving available in this market.

Season nudges, PCS surges. Summer construction and tourism hiring, the military’s May-through-August PCS wave, and the fall rush to beat winter all firm the northbound market; deep winter softens it for anyone whose vehicle is cold-ready. And as everywhere, two vehicles on one booking beat two bookings — the second car shares paperwork, port handling, and often deck space on the same sailing.

Leaving Alaska: The Southbound Story

Half our Anchorage work runs the other way — assignments ending, retirements heading to warmer winters, families following orders south — and the southbound trip has a few wrinkles of its own. The chain reverses cleanly: local pickup, the sailing to Tacoma, and a highway leg to anywhere in the Lower 48, all still one booking. The empty-vehicle and quarter-tank rules apply identically at this end, and winter departures need the same healthy battery the northbound trip did — the vessel deck in January is no place for a no-start.

Two southbound-specific tips: first, if your Lower 48 destination is flexible by even a hundred miles — a relative’s house near a major corridor versus a small town off one — tell us, because the highway leg prices very differently from a hub than from a spur. Second, selling the car south instead of shipping it is sometimes the right answer, and we’ll say so: for an older vehicle worth a few thousand dollars, the combined legs can approach its value, and an honest broker admits when the math argues for a for-sale sign instead of a booking.

PCS to JBER: Military Vehicle Shipping Done Right

Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson moves thousands of service members through Anchorage, and their vehicles are a fixture of the northbound and southbound sailings — which means we’ve built our process around the realities of a PCS rather than treating it like a civilian move with extra paperwork.

What that looks like in practice: we work backward from your report date the way we’d work backward from a sailing, because now there are two clocks; we document the shipment thoroughly for any reimbursement or entitlement claim; and when orders change your dates — the one constant of military life — we re-time the legs instead of restarting the booking. Shipping a POV at personal expense alongside a government-arranged one, sending a second family vehicle, or timing a car to arrive after your housing does: all standard requests, all handled weekly. The full picture is on our military shipping page, or call the Anchorage line and talk to someone who has walked a hundred families through the same move.

Eagle River and the Mat-Su Valley

Wasilla, Palmer, Eagle River, and Chugiak are standard delivery territory — the Glenn Highway makes them an easy last leg from the port. Winter changes the math on residential streets, though: an unplowed cul-de-sac in January means meeting the driver at a main-road lot, which every Alaskan already assumed.

Kenai, Soldotna, and Girdwood

The Seward Highway south is one of the more beautiful delivery routes in American trucking — and one where weather and avalanche control can close the road outright in winter. Peninsula deliveries run fine year-round; they just carry a built-in day of slack from November through March, and we schedule them that way from the start.

The Fairbanks connector

Vehicles continuing to the Interior move up the Parks Highway corridor by truck or on the Alaska Railroad’s vehicle service — figure $400–$700 and one to three extra days from Anchorage. In deep winter we’ll talk honestly about timing: minus-forty in Fairbanks is not the week to ship a car with a tired battery.

Anchorage Car Shipping FAQ

Common Questions

How much does it cost to ship a car to or from Anchorage?
Alaska shipping combines a land leg and an ocean leg, so totals run higher than any Lower 48 route: roughly $1,600–$2,300 all-in between Anchorage and the Seattle–Tacoma area, $2,000–$2,900 to or from California and the Southwest, and $2,200–$3,300 to or from the Midwest and East Coast for a sedan. Enclosed-equivalent (container) service costs more. A free quote breaks out both legs for your exact route.
How long does shipping to Alaska really take?
Plan in weeks, not days. The land leg to Tacoma runs 1–7 days depending on where you start, sailings to Anchorage depart on set weekly schedules and take about 4–8 days at sea including port handling, and local delivery adds a day or two. Door to door: figure 1–2 weeks from the West Coast and 2–3.5 weeks from the East Coast — and booking around the sailing schedule matters more than booking early in the week.
Why can't I put anything inside the car? I could on my last Lower 48 shipment.
The ocean leg is the reason. Maritime carriers enforce a strict empty-vehicle rule — no personal items at all, fuel at a quarter tank or less, and vehicles are inspected at the port before acceptance. A trunk full of boxes that a highway carrier might wave through will get your car refused at the terminal, and a refused car misses its sailing. Ship belongings separately; ship the car empty.
Is winter shipping to Anchorage safe for the vehicle?
Yes, with prep. The vessels and railcars are routine in winter; the vehicle itself needs to be ready for the cold soak on arrival: antifreeze rated to at least -40°, a healthy battery (cold kills weak ones fast), winter-rated washer fluid, and tires appropriate for the season. Weather can add a day or two to sailings and local delivery in the deep winter months.
Wouldn't driving the ALCAN or hiring a driveaway be cheaper?
Usually not, once you price it honestly: 2,200+ miles from Seattle through Canada means fuel, four to six nights of lodging, meals, border crossings, wear on the vehicle, and a week of your time — or a driveaway service whose costs land in the same range as ocean shipping while putting thousands of hard miles on your odometer. The ALCAN is a bucket-list drive worth doing on purpose; as a freight solution, the barge wins.
Do you handle military PCS moves to JBER?
Constantly — Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson drives a big share of Anchorage vehicle traffic. We work with PCS timelines and report dates, document everything for reimbursement claims where applicable, and if orders shift your dates (they do), we re-time the legs rather than charging you to start over.
Can you deliver beyond Anchorage — Wasilla, Kenai, Fairbanks?
Yes. The Mat-Su Valley (Wasilla, Palmer, Eagle River) is standard local delivery from the port. Kenai and Soldotna run as an overland leg down the Seward and Sterling highways, and Fairbanks moves by highway or rail up the Parks corridor — figure $400–$700 and an extra 1–3 days for the Fairbanks leg.
